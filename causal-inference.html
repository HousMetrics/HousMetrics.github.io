Causal Inference

A simple exploration of how causal relationships can be reasoned about, how confounding can distort interpretation, and how linear regression can be used to estimate effects more carefully.

Why causal inference matters

In machine learning, a linear regression model can be used to predict data points when the data is assumed to have a linear trend. Another use case is to determine whether a relationship between variables is not just predictive but plausibly causal.

→
Outcome

A direct causal relationship can be represented as a simple directional flow from variable to outcome.

In reality, there are often more variables and more intricate complexities lurking around the corner. A classic example is the relationship between a seasonal driver and two observed outcomes, where correlation may look strong even when the true causal story is more subtle.

Confounding

Consider a situation where a confounder influences both the treatment variable and the outcome. This can make two variables appear strongly related even when one does not directly cause the other.

The confounder affects both the variable and the outcome, creating a misleading association.

A useful illustration is the example of ice cream sales and shark attacks. Both rise during summer, so the correlation between them may look high even though the underlying causal mechanism is a third variable—warm weather—driving both. 

shark_attacks = 3 * summer_indicator + epsilon_sharks
ice_cream_sales = 2 * summer_indicator + epsilon_sales

In this setup, the error terms reflect noise, but the shared seasonal pattern can create a misleadingly high Pearson correlation. If we regress shark attacks on ice cream sales alone, we may infer a strong relationship that is not truly causal. 

X = ice_cream_sales.reshape(-1, 1)
y = shark_attacks
model = LinearRegression()
model.fit(X, y)

print("Intercept:", model.intercept_)
print("Slope:", model.coef_[0])

Intercept: 0.31
Slope: 1.29

This can be misleadingly interpreted as "for every ice cream sale there will be 1.29 shark attacks." However, the true causal story is different: the apparent relationship is confounded by the summer factor.

When we include the confounder in the model, the estimated relationship changes substantially. This illustrates a core lesson of causal inference: correlation is not enough; we need to reason about the data-generating process.
